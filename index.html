Polarimeter: how a chiral compound turns plane-polarized light

A simplified model of a sodium-lamp polarimeter. Change the sample and watch the plane of polarization twist as the light passes through the tube.

  1. The light's oscillating electric field pushes the electrons of a chiral molecule along its twisted framework.
  2. The push shifts charge (electric dipole) and also circulates it (magnetic dipole). In the mirror-image molecule the circulation is reversed.
  3. Left- and right-circularly polarized light therefore see slightly different refractive indices, nL ≠ nR.
  4. Plane-polarized light is the sum of equal L and R components. When one gets ahead, the sum tilts.
  5. The plane leaves turned by α = (π l / λ)(nL − nR). The enantiomer turns it by −α.

1. The bench

Light from a monochromatic sodium lamp (589 nm) is unpolarized: many planes at once. The polarizer keeps one plane. Inside the sample the plane turns steadily with distance. The analyzer passes the part of the wave along its axis, so the detector is brightest when φ = α. Dials show the view from the detector looking back toward the lamp. White light would not do: [α] changes with wavelength, so each color would turn by a different amount.

2. Why the plane turns

The three dials show the same light at successive points as it travels through the sample (left to right), seen end-on from the detector. Plane-polarized light equals right-circular (R, clockwise) plus left-circular (L, counterclockwise) light of half the amplitude each; their sum (black) oscillates along one line. The chiral sample slows one component more than the other, so the two arrows fall out of step and the line they trace tilts. The mirror-image sample slows the other component, so the tilt goes the other way. In the racemic mixture the two effects cancel exactly.

3. The molecule's side of it

Cartoon model: an electron (blue) constrained to a helical path. The field E pushes it along the axis, which gives an induced electric dipole parallel to E. Because the path is a helix, the electron also circulates around the axis and creates a magnetic moment m. The mirror-image helix circulates the opposite way, so m reverses relative to E, and with it the sign of the rotation. An achiral molecule has no such helical path, so there is no net coupling and no rotation. In this model the left-handed helix stands for the (+) form. Real (+)/(−) labels cannot be read from helix handedness, and a real solution has molecules in every orientation, which this picture does not show.
